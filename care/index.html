Flowerhorn care guide

A flowerhorn is a hardy fish that asks for a few things done well: a big tank, strong filtration, warm clean water and a sensible diet. Here is the whole picture, then the details.

What flowerhorn care comes down to

Flowerhorns have a reputation as tough fish, and it is mostly earned. They eat almost anything and shrug off small mistakes. That toughness is also the trap. A flowerhorn will keep eating and greeting you in a tank that is slowly failing, and by the time it hides, darkens or stops eating, the problem has usually been building for weeks.

Almost everything we see go wrong with flowerhorns traces back to four things: a tank that is too small, filtration that can’t keep up with a big eater, water changes that slipped, and too much food. Get those right and most of the rest follows, including the color, the kok and the personality.

Flowerhorn quick care sheet

These are the ranges we work with. References vary a little; the guides below explain each number.

Tank sizeAt least 75 gallons (284 L) for one adult; 90–125 gallons (340–473 L) for large males
Temperature80–86°F (27–30°C)
pHAbout 7.0–8.0
HardnessModerately hard, roughly 8–20 dGH
FiltrationHeavy: a canister or sump rated well above the tank volume
Water changes25–30% weekly; ammonia and nitrite at zero, nitrate kept low
DietQuality flowerhorn or cichlid pellet as the staple, frozen or fresh treats a few times a week
LifespanAbout 10–12 years
Adult sizeRoughly 12–16 in (30–40 cm)
Tank matesBest kept alone

A weekly care routine

A flowerhorn tank needs little time, but it needs it every week:

  • Daily: feed one to three small meals, check the thermometer, and look at your fish. Appetite, color and whether it comes to the glass tell you more than any test kit.
  • Weekly: change 25–30% of the water with a siphon that pulls waste off the bottom, and test ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH.
  • Every two to four weeks: rinse the mechanical filter pads in removed tank water, never straight tap water, and wipe the glass.
  • Every few months: check heater and filter parts, replace worn pads, and restock food before it goes stale.

Keep a log

A notebook with dates, test results and anything odd makes problems obvious long before your fish shows them.

Where new keepers go wrong

  • Buying the tank for the fish they have now. A 3-inch juvenile can pass 10 inches within about a year. Plan for the adult.
  • Adding the fish before the tank is cycled. Ammonia is more toxic in warm, alkaline water, which is exactly what flowerhorns live in.
  • Feeding for the begging, not the fish. A flowerhorn will always ask for more. Leftover food fouls the water fast.
  • Adding a tank mate. It rarely ends well. Our tank mates guide explains why.

Questions about flowerhorn care

Are flowerhorns easy to take care of?

They are hardy and forgiving of small mistakes, but they are not low-maintenance. They need a large tank, heavy filtration and weekly water changes for their whole 10 to 12 year life.

How often should I change my flowerhorn’s water?

Change about 25 to 30 percent every week. Heavily fed or crowded tanks may need more, and test results are the best guide.

Can a flowerhorn live with other fish?

Flowerhorns are highly territorial and are best kept alone. Most attempts at tank mates end with injured or dead fish.
