Flowerhorn diseases and how to treat them

Flowerhorns are tough fish, and when one gets sick the water is usually behind it. Here is how to read the symptoms, what the common problems are, and what keepers use to treat them.

Start with the water, every time

Veterinary references list the same root causes for most aquarium fish disease: stress, poor water quality, overcrowding and skipping quarantine. Flowerhorns fit that pattern closely. They eat a lot, produce a lot of waste and live in warm water, so a missed water change or a struggling filter shows up in the fish fast.

Before you reach for any medication, do three things:

  1. Test

    Check ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH and temperature with a liquid test kit. You are looking for zero ammonia and nitrite, low nitrate, pH around 7.0–8.0 and a steady 80–86°F (27–30°C). Our water and temperature guide explains each number.

  2. Change water

    Do a sizable water change with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water, and clean mechanical filter media in old tank water so you don’t wipe out beneficial bacteria.

  3. Observe

    Watch the fish for a day. Note appetite, color, fins, breathing, where it sits and what its waste looks like. Many mild problems improve with clean water alone.

About doses

We name treatments keepers commonly use, but we don’t give doses. Follow the product label exactly, and for a valuable or seriously ill fish, talk to a veterinarian who treats fish.

Hole in the head (HITH / HLLE)

Hole in the head, also called head and lateral line erosion (HLLE), is the disease large cichlid keepers worry about most. It starts as small pale pits around the face and forehead and can spread along the lateral line on the body. Advanced cases develop deeper, open lesions.

Its cause is still debated, and it is probably more than one thing. The factors most often cited are:

  • Poor water quality and inadequate filtration, especially long-term high organic waste.
  • Intestinal flagellates such as Hexamita and Spironucleus, which have long been blamed. Some fish with HITH turn out not to carry them, so they are a suspect, not a certainty.
  • Diet: a monotonous or stale diet, or a fish that is underfed.
  • Activated carbon: studies in marine fish linked carbon filtration to HLLE. Whether the same applies to freshwater cichlids is unclear.
  • Chronic stress from bullying tank mates, temperature swings or pH swings.

What keepers do: improve water quality first with larger, more frequent water changes and stronger biological filtration; pull carbon out of the filter; offer a fresher, more varied diet; and remove any source of stress. When flagellates are suspected, metronidazole is the medication most commonly used, often mixed into food so the fish takes it in. Some keepers use combination products such as metronidazole with praziquantel. A fish vet can confirm parasites under a microscope.

Caught early, HITH often stops progressing and pits can fill in, though severe lesions may leave permanent scars.

Swim bladder problems and bloat

The swim bladder controls buoyancy. When something goes wrong, a flowerhorn may float at the surface, struggle to leave the bottom, tilt head-down, or roll onto its side. Bloat is a swollen belly, and in flowerhorns the two often show up together.

Common causes: overfeeding, especially rich treats; constipation; swallowing air while gulping floating pellets; poor water quality; internal parasites; and bacterial infection. Short-body or “balloon” flowerhorns are more prone to swim bladder trouble because their compressed bodies crowd the internal organs.

What keepers do: check and correct the water, then stop feeding for a few days, which is the most common first step. When feeding resumes, offer smaller portions of soaked pellets. Some keepers use Epsom salt baths or treatments for constipation. White, stringy waste with bloat and a poor appetite points toward internal parasites, where metronidazole-based treatments are commonly used. If the fish doesn’t improve, a vet may take an X-ray and prescribe antibiotics for an internal infection.

Warning sign

A swollen body with scales lifting outward like a pinecone is dropsy, a sign of serious internal illness. Move the fish to a hospital tank and contact a vet.

Prevention is simple: don’t overfeed. Our feeding guide covers portions and schedules by age.

Ich (white spot)

Ich, caused by the parasite Ichthyophthirius multifiliis, looks like grains of salt on the body and fins. Infected fish flash (rub against objects), clamp their fins, breathe faster and go off food. It often follows a temperature drop or shipping, or arrives with new fish.

The key to treating ich is its life cycle. The parasite lives under the skin, drops off, multiplies on surfaces, and releases free-swimming stages that look for a new host. Only that free-swimming stage is vulnerable to treatment, so treatment must be repeated for long enough to catch every generation. University of Florida extension research notes the cycle runs faster in warm water, which matters for flowerhorns kept in the 80s°F: the parasite moves quickly, and so should you.

What keepers use: aquarium salt, and commercial ich medications based on formalin, malachite green or copper. Remove carbon during treatment so it doesn’t absorb the medication, vacuum the substrate, and keep treating for the full course on the label even after the spots disappear.

Fin rot, fungus and cloudy eye

Fin rot

Fins look frayed, ragged or eaten back, often with white, red or dark edges. The usual causes are dirty water, injury from decor or a tank mate, and stress. Clean water often halts mild cases. For progressing cases, keepers commonly use antibacterial products such as erythromycin or kanamycin. Fast-moving white patches on the body, mouth or fins can be columnaris, a bacterial disease common in warm-water fish that needs prompt treatment.

Fungal infections

Fungus such as Saprolegnia appears as grayish-white, cotton-like tufts, usually on a wound or damaged area rather than on healthy skin. Treat the cause (the injury and the water) as well as the fungus. Methylene blue and commercial antifungal products are the usual choices. If the “fungus” spreads quickly, suspect columnaris instead.

Cloudy eye

A milky film over the eye. One cloudy eye usually means injury, which is common in flowerhorns that charge the glass or crash into decor; it often clears on its own in clean water. Both eyes cloudy suggests water quality or infection. Fix the water first, then consider an antibacterial treatment if it doesn’t improve. A bulging eye or a cloudy eye that lasts for weeks is worth a vet visit.

Stress, lethargy and dark color

A flowerhorn wears its mood. A healthy fish is bright, curious and pushy at feeding time. A stressed one darkens, shows heavy black bars or blotches, sits at the bottom or in a corner, clamps its fins and ignores food. Its kok often shrinks too (see kok growth).

Common triggers include:

  • A new tank, shipping or rearranged decor. Some darkening in the first week or two is normal.
  • Water problems: ammonia, nitrite, a failing heater or a pH swing.
  • A tank mate, a fish visible through a divider, or its own reflection. See tank mates.
  • Heavy foot traffic, loud noise or constant tapping on the glass.
  • The early stage of any of the diseases above.

Gasping at the surface is an emergency: check temperature, oxygen and ammonia immediately and do a water change. Otherwise, fix what you can find, keep the routine steady and give the fish time. If dark color and poor appetite last more than a few days in good water, look closer for signs of illness.

Hospital tank basics and prevention

A hospital tank lets you treat one fish without dosing your whole display tank or harming its filter bacteria. It doubles as a quarantine tank for new fish. For a flowerhorn, a simple setup works:

  • A bare tank of roughly 20–40 gallons (75–150 L), sized to the fish.
  • A heater, a thermometer and a tight lid. Sick flowerhorns can still jump.
  • A sponge filter kept running in the main tank so it is already cycled when needed.
  • No carbon and no decor, apart from something smooth if the fish needs cover.
  • Water taken from the main tank at first, so temperature and pH match.

Medicated water still needs changing, and a small tank fouls quickly, so test often. Many keepers quarantine new fish for two to four weeks before they join any established system.

Prevention in short

Keep the tank big enough, the filter strong and the water changes regular (the tank setup guide covers filtration). Feed a quality staple without overfeeding, avoid feeder fish from unknown sources, keep flowerhorns on their own, and quarantine new arrivals. Fish that live that way rarely need this page.

Questions about flowerhorn diseases

Why is my flowerhorn turning black?

Dark color and black bars usually mean stress. Common causes are a new tank, poor water quality, a temperature change, a tank mate or early illness, so test the water first and look for other symptoms.

How do you treat hole in the head in flowerhorns?

Keepers start by improving water quality and filtration, removing carbon, varying the diet and reducing stress. When intestinal parasites are suspected, metronidazole is commonly used, following the product label or a vet’s directions.

Why is my flowerhorn floating upside down?

That points to a swim bladder problem, often from overfeeding, constipation or poor water. Check the water, fast the fish for a few days, and see a vet if it doesn’t improve.

Why is my flowerhorn not eating?

Stress from a move, water problems or illness are the usual reasons. A newly arrived fish may refuse food for several days, but a settled fish that stops eating needs a water test and a close look for symptoms.

Can I use aquarium salt for a flowerhorn?

Aquarium salt is commonly used for ich and for mild stress or wounds, and flowerhorns generally tolerate it. Follow the label directions and remove it with water changes once treatment ends.
