Hybrid cichlids

The Red Texas cichlid

Despite the name, it isn’t a Texas cichlid at all. It’s a hybrid named for the fish it resembles, with a parentage story that even dedicated sources can’t agree on.

What a Red Texas cichlid is

The Red Texas cichlid is a hybrid, not a species, and like most hybrid cichlids it has no scientific name. It is bred for a solid red-to-orange body with the spotted, speckled look of a true Texas cichlid, and it is sold worldwide as an aquarium fish rather than caught wild. It belongs in the same broad category as the blood parrot and its relatives covered on this site, even though it is not related to the flowerhorn.

Also sold asRed Texas, Red Devil Texas, Super Red Texas
Commonly described asTexas cichlid (male) × red parrot (female)
Adult sizeSources disagree: 8 to 10 in (20 to 25 cm) by one breeder profile, up to 12 in (30 cm) by a hobby care sheet
Tank size75 gal (284 L) minimum for one; 125 gal (473 L) or more with tank mates
Temperature68 to 82°F (20 to 28°C)
pHAbout 6.5 to 7.5
TemperamentHardy, territorial and often described as one of the more aggressive parrot-type hybrids

A name that oversells the truth

Start with what the name doesn’t mean. Wikipedia’s own entry for the real Texas cichlid (Herichthys cyanoguttatus), the only cichlid native to the United States, says plainly that the “red Texas cichlid” sold in stores “is not a true Texas cichlid” and that the name comes only from its resemblance to that species. The same entry notes a second, unrelated mix-up: the “green Texas cichlid” often sold under that name is usually Herichthys carpintis, the pearlscale cichlid, a different species whose native range doesn’t even reach Texas.

So two different “Texas cichlids” on sale at any given store may have nothing to do with the wild fish from the Rio Grande, and the real Texas cichlid contributes only half the ancestry of the red one, and only as a father, never a mother.

The parentage dispute

Everyone selling this fish agrees on one parent: a male Texas cichlid. Past that, the story gets harder to pin down, and the two detailed profiles we found don’t fully agree with each other.

  • Aquarium Glaser, a German wholesaler that ships direct from Southeast Asian farms, describes the mother as a “Red Parrot” that is itself “allegedly” a cross of the redhead cichlid (Vieja melanurus) and the red devil cichlid (Amphilophus labiatus) — hedging its own source with that one word.
  • A widely syndicated hobby care sheet instead names the mother’s parents as Paraneetroplus synspilus and Amphilophus citrinellus.

The disagreement is smaller than it looks. “Redhead cichlid” is used in the trade for both Vieja melanurus and the very similar Vieja synspila (the species the second source calls Paraneetroplus synspilus, an older name for the same fish), and “red devil” gets applied loosely to both Amphilophus labiatus and the similar Amphilophus citrinellus (the Midas cichlid) in the same way hobbyists mix up the two devils. Once you account for that, both sources are describing the same cross — a redhead-type cichlid crossed with a devil- or Midas-type cichlid — to make the “red parrot,” which is then crossed again with a male Texas cichlid. That makes the Red Texas, at minimum, a three-species hybrid, and the exact pairing within each generation is simply not documented anywhere we could find. Treat any single, confident family tree for this fish, including this one, as the trade’s best guess rather than a verified pedigree.

One other wrinkle

“Red parrot” is also used on this site and elsewhere as a plain alias for the blood parrot cichlid (see blood parrot). The red parrot used to breed a Red Texas is a different, separate hybrid under the same loose name — one more case of parrot-hybrid names overlapping in the trade.

Where and when it was first bred

Aquarium Glaser’s stock notes date the cross to 2004, crediting an informant with the detail that the first specimens sold for roughly $4,000 each. That figure and date rest on a single named source rather than independent documentation, so we can’t verify it further, but it fits the pattern of other high-dollar, farm-bred color hybrids coming out of Southeast Asia in the same decade that produced the flowerhorn boom (see origins). Whatever the exact year, the fish has had two decades to spread through the wholesale trade, and today’s fish several generations removed from any founder stock.

What it looks like

Young fish are sold small, often 2 to 3 inches (5 to 8 cm), and arrive under-colored: the breeder profile we cite describes six-to-eight-centimeter juveniles as “still young” and not yet showing their final color. As they mature, expect:

  • A Texas-cichlid body shape, deeper and more elongated than a round blood parrot, inherited from the father’s side.
  • Iridescent spotting, the Texas cichlid’s signature green-to-blue pearl spots, scattered over a red or orange ground color instead of the wild fish’s olive-green.
  • Red-to-orange base color from the red parrot side, which is why the fish reads as “red” from across the store despite the spotting pattern underneath.
  • A normal, functional mouth in most individuals — unlike a blood parrot, the Texas-cichlid half of the cross generally keeps the jaw working properly, which also makes feeding easier.

Size and temperament

The two sources we found don’t agree on adult size. The breeder profile cites 8 to 10 inches (20 to 25 cm) at maturity. A separate hobby care sheet cites a flat 12 inches (30 cm) in its summary table, with juveniles reportedly adding at least 4 inches in the first six months, and it describes a shorter-bodied variant that tops out around 8 inches (20 cm). Until there’s better documentation, the safest plan is to size the tank for the larger figure and treat anything smaller as a pleasant surprise.

Temperament is more consistent across sources: this is a hardy, territorial, actively aggressive fish, in line with its Texas cichlid father, which the real species’ own Wikipedia entry describes as aggressive whether or not it is defending territory. One care sheet calls the Red Texas “one of the most aggressive cichlid species” in the hobby, warning that it will dig up plants and substrate and can injure or kill smaller tank mates. Buy one expecting a Texas cichlid’s attitude in a showier coat, not a mellow parrot.

Care and feeding

Care leans Central American cichlid, not flowerhorn or blood parrot:

  • Tank. 75 gallons (284 L) minimum for a single adult; 125 gallons (473 L) or more if it will share space, matching the housing a real Texas cichlid needs.
  • Filtration. Strong filtration rated well above the tank volume, plus weekly 25 to 30 percent water changes. This is a messy, digging fish.
  • Water. A comfortable range is 68 to 82°F (20 to 28°C) and pH 6.5 to 7.5, with zero ammonia and nitrite. See water and temperature for how to hold steady parameters.
  • Decor and lid. Sand or fine gravel the fish can move without harm, rock and driftwood anchored or buried rather than stacked loose, and a tight-fitting lid, since Central American cichlids are capable jumpers.

Feed a quality cichlid pellet sized to the fish as the staple, with frozen shrimp, krill and bloodworms as occasional treats, and some vegetable matter to round out the diet. Because most Red Texas cichlids keep a normal, functioning mouth, they generally don’t need the soaked or micro-pellet workarounds that a true blood parrot does; see feeding for color-enhancing food and portioning. Keep tank mates to similarly robust, similarly sized fish that can hold their own, and never house one with a flowerhorn or another territorial cichlid in anything but a very large tank; tank mates covers the general reasoning.

Is it really a mule?

Fertility claims for this fish contradict each other, sometimes within the same source. One widely cited care sheet calls the Red Texas “most likely infertile” in its summary table, comparing it to a mule — then, in its breeding section, describes pairs spawning 500 to 1,000 eggs, which is not what you’d expect from a sterile animal. That is a real contradiction, not a nuance we’re smoothing over, and we could not find a cleaner source to resolve it.

What’s consistent with the rest of the hybrid-cichlid trade, including the flowerhorn’s own blood parrot ancestor, is that female hybrids are more often fertile than males. If that pattern holds here too, occasional successful spawns alongside mostly infertile pairings would explain both claims without either source being simply wrong. Treat any Red Texas you buy as unlikely to breed true, and don’t count on consistent offspring if it does spawn.

Questions about the Red Texas cichlid

Is a Red Texas cichlid a real Texas cichlid?

No. Wikipedia's own Texas cichlid entry is explicit that the Red Texas is not a true Texas cichlid — it is a common Herichthys × Amphilophus cross named for its resemblance to Herichthys cyanoguttatus, the real Texas cichlid.

What is a Red Texas cichlid a cross of?

Sellers agree on a male Texas cichlid (Herichthys cyanoguttatus) crossed with a female red parrot. The red parrot side is itself a hybrid, usually given as a redhead cichlid crossed with a red devil or Midas cichlid, so the fish carries genes from at least three species.

How big does a Red Texas cichlid get?

Estimates differ. One wholesale breeder profile cites 8 to 10 inches (20 to 25 cm) at maturity; a hobby care sheet cites up to 12 inches, with a shorter-bodied form around 8 inches. Plan tank size for the larger figure.

Are Red Texas cichlids fertile?

Accounts conflict. Some care sheets call the cross most likely infertile, like a mule; others describe pairs spawning several hundred to a thousand eggs. As with many cichlid hybrids, females are more often fertile than males, but there is no solid consensus.
