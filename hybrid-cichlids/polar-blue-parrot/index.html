Hybrid cichlids

The polar blue parrot cichlid

Pale body, dark convict-style bars and a shock of blue. Ask three retailers what makes one and you will get three different family trees.

What a polar blue parrot is

The polar blue parrot cichlid is a small, round-bodied hybrid cichlid sold with a pale white-to-cream base, dark convict-style barring, and a patch or wash of iridescent blue. Like the blood parrot and its relatives covered on this site, it has no wild population and no scientific name of its own; what it does have, unusually for a parrot-type hybrid, is a real dispute over what two species actually made it.

Also sold asPolar blue convict parrot, polar parrot
Commonly described asConvict cichlid × blood parrot, though sources disagree (see below)
Adult sizeSources disagree: about 12 cm (4.7 in) to 5 to 7 in (13 to 18 cm) depending on the source

The parentage dispute

Most US retailers sell this fish as a straightforward cross. Aquatic Arts lists it as a tank-bred cross of the blood parrot with the convict cichlid (Amatitlania nigrofasciata), and ourAquariumLife’s care guide agrees, describing a male convict crossed with a female blood/parrot cichlid. That is the version you will see most often, including in Pet Zone’s own tanks.

Two other sources complicate it:

  • Quality Marine has published a different parentage for a fish of the same name. One Quality Marine news post calls it a combination of “Convict Cichlid and Blood Parrot-type” traits. A separate, older Quality Marine post for what it also calls the “Parrot Polar Blue Convict Hybrid” gives scientific names instead: Archocentrus nigrofasciatus (the convict cichlid) crossed with A. citrinellus, the Midas cichlid — no blood parrot in that family tree at all. The same wholesaler has, at different times, described the cross two different ways.
  • A UK retailer doubts the blood parrot story entirely. Maidenhead Aquatics’ fishkeeper.co.uk profile argues the fish more likely descends from selectively bred, short-bodied convict cichlids crossed with Honduran red point cichlids, a short-body trait convergent with (but separate from) the one seen in true parrots. Its reasoning: these fish stay convict-sized rather than growing toward a blood parrot’s larger frame, and — unlike sterile male blood parrot hybrids — they breed readily when paired, which points toward convict-family ancestry on both sides rather than a blood parrot parent.

We could not find a single documented cross that all sources agree on. What is consistent across every source is a convict cichlid somewhere in the mix; whether the other parent is a blood parrot, a Midas cichlid, or another short-bodied convict-relative depends on who is selling the fish. Treat any confident, single-sentence parentage claim for this fish, including the common “blood parrot × convict” one, as the trade’s best guess rather than a settled fact.

Name check

“Polar blue parrot” and “polar blue convict” are used interchangeably by sellers for the same fish. Don’t confuse it with the unrelated blood parrot’s own color forms, which are called platinum or panda parrots instead.

Where the name and the look come from

The “polar” in the name points at the fish’s coloring: a pale, near-white to cream base reminiscent of an arctic animal, set off by dark vertical convict-style bars and, in better-colored individuals, a shining patch or overall wash of electric blue. ourAquariumLife describes the blue as the fish’s signature feature and the reason for the name; fishkeeper.co.uk notes the strong blue tones specifically as evidence the fish leans more convict-and-red-point than blood parrot in ancestry, since that coloring doesn’t show up in true blood parrot lines.

Body shape is compact and rounded, closer to a short convict than to a full-sized blood parrot, with a mouth that (unlike a true blood parrot’s) generally closes and functions normally. Juveniles are hard to sex; maturing males typically grow thinner-bodied but longer with more elaborate fins, while females run shorter and thicker-bodied and develop an orange or yellow blush on the lower belly when carrying eggs.

Size: sources disagree by more than double

This is the sharpest disagreement we found. Fishkeeper.co.uk, the source most skeptical of blood parrot ancestry, caps the fish at about 12 cm (4.7 in) — essentially standard convict size, which it offers as supporting evidence for its own parentage theory. ourAquariumLife instead cites 3 to 4 inches typically, up to about 5 inches in ideal conditions. Aquatic Arts lists 4 to 5 inches. Quality Marine’s convict-×-Midas listing goes furthest, citing 5 to 7 inches “due to its parent lineage” — consistent with a Midas cichlid parent, which grows far larger than a convict.

The spread lines up with the parentage dispute above: the sources that describe a convict-heavy cross cite convict-like sizes, and the one source naming a Midas cichlid parent cites a noticeably larger fish. Until there is better documentation, size the tank for the larger end of whichever range applies to the fish in front of you, and don’t assume a 3-inch juvenile has finished growing.

Fertility, and what it hints about parentage

Every source we found describes this fish as a capable breeder. ourAquariumLife calls it an easy egg-layer: females lay 100 to 300 eggs on a flat surface or in a cave, the male fertilizes and guards them, and fry hatch in four to five days. Aquatic Arts reports successful spawning and fry-rearing as common among hobbyists, while noting fertility “can vary between individual fish due to hybrid origins.” A MonsterFishKeepers forum thread describes a keeper’s polar blue parrots readily coexisting and breeding alongside standard convicts.

That matters because it cuts against the most commonly cited parentage. True blood parrot hybrids are notorious for male infertility (see our blood parrot page), so a fish that breeds this easily and this consistently is behaving more like a convict-family cross than a blood-parrot-family one. We’re flagging that as a point in favor of the skeptical theory in the parentage dispute above, not as proof; none of the sources we found ran a genetic test, and “it breeds easily” is consistent with several of the competing family trees.

Care and tank setup

Care recommendations vary with how big each source expects the fish to get, but the ranges overlap enough to plan around:

  • Tank. 30 gallons (114 L) is the smallest figure cited, for a single pair. Given the size disagreement above, we’d plan for 40 to 55 gallons (150 to 208 L) for one adult and 75 gallons (284 L) or more for a small group, which matches the higher end of every source’s recommendation.
  • Filtration. Efficient filtration with some moderate current plus calmer resting areas; these are not fast-water fish, but they are messy for their size.
  • Water. Cited ranges run 75 to 85°F (24 to 29°C) and pH 6.5 to 8.0, with hardness commonly cited at 6 to 20 dKH — unusually tolerant of harder, more alkaline water than a flowerhorn or blood parrot. Keep ammonia and nitrite at zero regardless; see water and temperature for the general approach.
  • Decor. A soft, sandy substrate for digging and spawning pits, with rock and driftwood anchored rather than stacked loose. Caves for breeding pairs and for shy fish to retreat into.
  • Diet. Omnivorous and not picky: quality cichlid pellets or flake sized to the fish, plus frozen brine shrimp, bloodworms, mysis and krill, and occasional vegetable matter such as peas or spirulina-based foods. Because the mouth generally works normally, these fish don’t need the soaked or micro-pellet accommodations a true blood parrot does.

Tank mates

Sources describe the temperament consistently even where parentage doesn’t agree: territorial toward smaller fish, especially when breeding, but not built for serious fighting and generally manageable in a community of appropriately sized fish. ourAquariumLife recommends angelfish, other Central American cichlids, severums, corydoras and plecos as tank mates, and suggests keeping a small group of polar blue parrots together to spread out aggression. Avoid small fish such as guppies or platies, which several sources note get bullied or eaten outright, and avoid fish boisterous enough to out-compete these for food.

Because these fish will readily interbreed with standard convict cichlids when both sexes are present, keep that in mind if keeping a pure convict colony matters to you; see the ethics discussion on hybrid cichlids generally for why that bothers some keepers more than others.

Should you get one?

A polar blue parrot suits a keeper who wants a compact, colorful, relatively easy cichlid and doesn’t need a confirmed pedigree to enjoy one. It is a poor choice for anyone trying to keep a documented species line, given how unsettled its ancestry is, or for a tank stocked with small, delicate fish. Pet Zone carries polar blue parrots when available through flowerhorn.co. For the wider picture on crosses like this, see hybrid cichlids explained, or compare it with the blood parrot it’s most often said to descend from.

Questions about polar blue parrots

What is a polar blue parrot cichlid a cross of?

Most US retailers call it a blood parrot crossed with a convict cichlid. But Quality Marine has separately published it as a convict x Midas cross, and a UK retailer argues it is actually a short-bodied convict crossed with a Honduran red point cichlid, with no blood parrot involved at all. No source we found settles which is correct.

How big do polar blue parrots get?

Sources disagree by more than double. A UK retailer caps them at about 12 cm (4.7 in), most US sellers cite 3 to 5 inches, and one Quality Marine listing cites 5 to 7 inches. Plan for the smaller end unless your fish is clearly still growing past it.

Are polar blue parrots fertile?

Yes, generally, and that is one reason some sources doubt the blood parrot parentage. Unlike true blood parrot hybrids, whose males are usually sterile, polar blue parrots are regularly reported pairing, spawning and raising fry, which fits a convict-cichlid-heavy ancestry better than a blood-parrot-heavy one.

Can polar blue parrots live with convicts?

Generally yes, and they will interbreed with regular convicts if both sexes are present. Keepers report the two getting along, though there is no guarantee a polar blue will pair only with its own kind rather than a convict.
