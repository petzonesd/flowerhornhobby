Keeping and buying a flowerhorn in San Diego

San Diego is easy country for a flowerhorn. The tap water is already hard and alkaline, and the climate rarely fights your heater. Here is what to check anyway.

What San Diego tap water means for a flowerhorn

Flowerhorns carry Central American cichlid blood, and those fish live in warm, moderately hard, slightly alkaline water. San Diego hands you that for free: most local keepers fill from the tap, treat it, and never touch a pH bottle. The City of San Diego’s 2024 Annual Drinking Water Quality Report breaks results out by treatment plant.

Total hardnessPlant averages 216 to 230 ppm as calcium carbonate (12.6 to 13.4 grains per gallon). Full range 172 to 248 ppm, roughly 10 to 14 dGH.
AlkalinityPlant averages 110 to 123 ppm, roughly 6 to 7 dKH.
pHPlant averages 8.12 to 8.31. Range 7.28 to 8.76.
Total dissolved solidsPlant averages 479 to 571 ppm. Range 367 to 614 ppm.
Chloramine residualDistribution average 1.73 ppm, up to 3.80 ppm.

Line those up against our water and temperature page and the picture is good. Hardness of 10 to 14 dGH sits inside the 8 to 20 dGH a flowerhorn likes, and 6 to 7 dKH buffers pH against crashing between water changes. Only pH runs slightly above our stated 7.0 to 8.0, and that is not worth fixing. A fish in stable water at 8.2 is healthy; the same fish bounced around by pH-down is not.

TDS meters

San Diego tap reads around 450 to 570 ppm before a fish has swum in it. A TDS pen is not a useful waste test here. Test nitrate instead.

The numbers also move, which is the part newcomers miss. Most of the supply is imported through the San Diego County Water Authority as a blend of Colorado River and State Water Project water, with Carlsbad desalination in the mix, and the proportions shift with rainfall and demand. Water the city buys already treated ranged from 60.4 to 243 ppm hardness in 2024, and the city’s own water quality page quotes a typical figure nearer 16 grains per gallon (276 ppm), varying by demand and neighborhood. Buy a GH and KH kit and test your own tap twice a year.

Chloramine is the one thing you must handle. San Diego disinfects with chloramine rather than free chlorine. Free chlorine gasses off if a bucket stands overnight; chloramine does not, and aerating will not remove it. Use a conditioner whose label says it neutralizes chloramine (most modern ones do) and dose the full volume of new water every time, including top-offs. Breaking the chloramine bond releases ammonia, so a conditioner that detoxifies ammonia too is sensible on a big change. The city’s water quality FAQ says it plainly: the disinfectants used to treat the water are harmful to fish. Ammonia burn from an untreated fill mimics several things on our disease page, and it is entirely avoidable.

Heaters, the marine layer and inland summers

Our range is 80 to 86°F (27 to 30°C). San Diego makes that easy to hold, not automatic.

Coastal winters are mild outdoors and cooler indoors than people expect. Plenty of older apartments and beach-area houses have minimal heating and are not heated at night, so a living room sits in the low 60s°F from December through March. That is a 20°F gap for your heater to close, continuously.

Heater wattage is about that gap, not the target by itself. Our rule is 3 to 5 watts per gallon split across two heaters: in an unheated room take the upper end, and two units mean a stuck-off heater is an inconvenience rather than a dead fish. On a 75 gallon tank that is roughly 225 to 375 watts. The tank calculator sizes it for your volume, and the tank setup guide covers placement.

Garages and unheated back rooms are where local keepers get caught. A San Diego garage tracks outside air far more closely than the house does: cold enough in January to run a heater flat out, hot enough in August to make it irrelevant. Insulate the stand, put the heaters on a controller with an alarm, and check the temperature every morning for the first month.

The inland difference

El Cajon, Santee, Escondido and Ramona run hotter in summer and cooler at night. The same tank behaves differently ten miles inland.

Summer is the opposite problem. Inland valleys regularly see highs in the upper 90s, and many coastal homes have no air conditioning because they normally do not need it. A closed-up room during a September heat event can push a tank past 86°F, and warm water holds less oxygen just when the fish needs more. What works, in order: lights off during the hottest hours, a clip fan across the surface with the lid cracked (usually worth 2 to 4°F), and extra aeration. Frozen bottles swing the temperature, so treat them as a last resort. Condition every top-off.

Buying locally versus having one shipped

Both work, and they fail differently. The general version is in our buying guide; this is the San Diego part.

Buying in person

  • Ask for the fish to be fed while you watch. One that ignores food will not improve in your car.
  • Ask how long it has been in the store and what it eats, including any color food.
  • Check the head for pitting, eyes for cloudiness and fins for fraying.
  • Put your hand near the glass. You want a fish that comes at you, not one sulking in a back corner.
  • Drive straight home. Summer traffic plus a bag on a back seat is a real risk: bring a cooler, keep the bag dark, skip the errands. Even a 30 minute drive deserves a temperature match.

Having one shipped

  • Far more choice. If you want a specific strain, grade or size, a local shelf may not have it.
  • Insist on dated photos or video of the exact fish and a written live arrival guarantee.
  • Ask what day it ships, by what service, and whether it sits at a facility over a weekend.
  • Our own fish ship nationwide from flowerhorn.co, the same stock you would see at Mid-City.
  • Read shipping and acclimation before the box arrives, not after.
  • A San Diego seller shipping to a San Diego address means a short hop and an easy conversation if something goes wrong.

Either way, have the tank cycled and at temperature before you buy.

Clubs and community

San Diego has a real aquarium club scene, and it is worth using. Club people have kept fish through power cuts, heat waves and moves.

The San Diego Tropical Fish Society is the general freshwater club. Public meetings are on the second Sunday of each month at 6pm in Room 101 of the Casa del Prado in Balboa Park, and the club says guests thinking about joining are welcome. It runs shows and auctions through the year. It is broad-interest rather than a cichlid group, so expect plants and goldfish alongside the big fish.

The Koi Club of San Diego is the pond side, and its annual show at the Del Mar Fairgrounds is the big one on the local calendar. A flowerhorn is not a koi, but the overlap is real: large-fish filtration, pond-grade equipment and people who think in hundreds of gallons. Our Mid-City store is a monster fish and koi store for the same reason.

Schedules and show dates change, so check a club’s own site before driving across the county. Local fishkeeping groups on social platforms are active too. Treat private sales there with the caution our buying guide applies to any marketplace listing.

If you can no longer keep your flowerhorn

People move, tanks break, and a 10 inch fish needing 75 gallons is not a small thing to hand off.

Do not release it. Not into a pond, a lake, a creek, a storm drain or the bay. The California Department of Fish and Wildlife asks people not to let aquarium pets loose, calling it inhumane and harmful, because released animals can displace native species. For the fish it is a slow death: a tropical hybrid in a San Diego waterway faces cold winter water and no food it recognizes. Flushing is no better.

  1. Call a local fish store first

    Phone before you load the fish into the car. Ask whether they take surrenders, in what condition and when to bring it. Stores that handle large cichlids are likeliest to say yes, but no one can promise space on the day.

  2. Ask the clubs and local groups

    Post in a San Diego fishkeeping group or raise it at a club meeting. Large cichlids move quickly among people who already own a big enough tank.

  3. Offer the tank with the fish

    The biggest barrier is that the new keeper needs 75 gallons standing empty. Passing on the whole setup makes an adopter out of someone who would otherwise decline.

  4. Be honest about the fish

    Give its real size, age if you know it, what it eats, whether it bites and any health history. A flowerhorn rehomed with accurate information stays rehomed.

Taking one in? A fish that has moved house needs the same quiet settling-in period as one out of a box, covered on our acclimation page.

Questions from San Diego keepers

Is San Diego tap water good for flowerhorns?

Yes, and it is one of the easier tap supplies in the country for this fish. Hardness of roughly 10 to 14 dGH and pH around 8 suit a flowerhorn well. Treat for chloramine and you are done.

Do I need RO water or a water softener for a flowerhorn here?

No. Reverse osmosis water is too soft to use unremineralized, and a household softener swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium. Use dechlorinated tap.

Does a flowerhorn need a heater in San Diego?

Yes. Coastal rooms often sit in the low 60s°F overnight in winter, well below the 80 to 86°F range. Plan on 3 to 5 watts per gallon split across two heaters.
