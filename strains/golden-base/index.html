Golden Base, Golden Monkey and faders

Buy a dark little fish, wait a few months, and end up with a gold one. The fade is the whole appeal of the Golden Base, and the reason so many keepers start here.

What “Golden Base” means

Golden Base is one of the founding flowerhorn families from the Malaysian breeding boom, grouped with Kamfa and Zhen Zhu in most histories of the fish. An adult is a warm gold to yellow fish, often washed with orange or red on the face, chest and kok, with or without a line of dark “flower” markings along the side.

What makes the group unusual is how it gets there. Golden Base juveniles typically darken to charcoal or near-black, then lose that black over the following months as the gold underneath comes through. Fish that do this are called faders, and in everyday store talk “Golden Base” and “fader” are used almost interchangeably.

Names you will see

Golden Base, GB, fader, Golden Trimac and Golden Monkey all turn up on labels for gold flowerhorns. They overlap, and sellers do not use them consistently.

Strictly, “fader” describes a behavior, not a strain. Keepers report fading in fish from several lines, and a Golden Base is best thought of as a fader that ends up gold or orange. You will also see Golden Trimac, which older hobby writing describes as a rawer cross that ends up looking like a gold-and-red trimac cichlid. For how all of these fit together, our strains overview and the strains table on the breeding page are the place to start.

The fade, month by month

No two faders run on the same clock, but the general sequence keepers describe is consistent:

  1. Fry and small juveniles

    Young fish look like most flowerhorn fry: grey to tan, with dark bars or blotches and a little early pearling. At this stage a future Golden Base can be hard to tell from any other pet-grade flowerhorn.

  2. Going dark

    As juveniles grow, many darken until much of the body is smoky grey or black. New owners often assume something is wrong. In a fader, this dark phase is expected.

  3. The black starts to lift

    Gold or orange patches appear and spread, in a different order on every fish. For a while the fish looks blotchy and uneven, which is normal and temporary.

  4. Adult color settles

    Most of the black is gone, leaving yellow, gold, orange or red. Some fish keep a clean line of flower markings; others lose most of their pattern.

How long does it take? Keepers report anything from about a month to more than two years for a single fish to finish, and a few stall partway for a long time. “Several months” is the typical answer, but patience is the only reliable advice. Young fish tend to change faster than older ones.

Don’t chase it

No food or additive reliably triggers a fade. Good water and a normal diet let it happen on the fish’s own schedule.

Color can keep shifting after the fade too. Owners regularly report a Golden Base whose red softens to orange over a few months. That is usually development, not decline, as long as the fish is eating and active.

Why faders change color

Nobody has studied flowerhorn fading directly, but there is a close, well-documented parallel in one of the fish most often listed among its ancestors. Some Midas cichlids, which our origins page lists as a likely source of the flowerhorn’s gold and orange tones, start life with the normal grey, barred pattern and later lose their black pigment cells, turning gold or even white. A 2013 study in BMC Genomics describes this change as starting anywhere from a few months old to several years, progressing unevenly, and sometimes leaving dark patches that persist for years.

That is almost exactly what flowerhorn keepers describe in faders: a variable start, a blotchy middle stage and occasional stalls. It is a reasonable guess, though not a proven one, that Golden Base lines carry a similar trait that breeders concentrated by keeping the fish that finished brightest. Keepers also note that some Vieja cichlids, another ancestor group, are slow to reach adult color.

What this means for you: the dark phase is part of the package, not a quality problem, and a fish that is still dark at purchase is a bet on the color underneath.

Where Golden Monkey fits

The Golden Monkey name has two lives, which is why it causes so much confusion.

The original line

  • Described in hobby accounts as a separate, prized Malaysian line, not a simple fader
  • Remembered for deep color, heavy pearling on the head, a thick, chubby face and inset eyes
  • The fish behind the record-setting sale mentioned in our origins timeline
  • Widely said to have been hard to reproduce true, even for its breeders

The name on today’s labels

  • Often used loosely for gold, yellow or orange flowerhorns of mixed background
  • Overlaps heavily with Golden Base in the trade
  • Survives inside other names: Malau and Kamalau both trace to the Cantonese word for “monkey”
  • Rarely backed by any documentation of the original line

Our own strains table groups Golden Base and Golden Monkey in a single row for exactly this reason. If a seller asks a premium for a “Golden Monkey,” ask what they mean: a named line with known parents, or simply a gold fish. The monkey-faced lines that grew out of this history are covered in our Kamalau guide.

Why Golden Base is a common first flowerhorn

When someone comes into the store wanting their first flowerhorn and doesn’t want to spend show-fish money, a Golden Base is often where the conversation ends up. The reasons are practical:

  • Hardy. Golden Base fish have a reputation as sturdy, forgiving flowerhorns that handle the learning curve of a first big cichlid well.
  • Affordable. They generally cost less than Kamfa, SRD or Thai Silk fish of similar size. See our flowerhorn price guide for how strains compare.
  • A decent kok. They usually won’t match a top Kamfa, but many males grow a respectable head.
  • The fade itself. Watching a fish change from charcoal to gold over a few months keeps people hooked. It feels like getting a new fish without buying one.
  • Full flowerhorn personality. They are every bit as interactive as pricier strains, which is really what most people want. Our page on flowerhorns as pet fish explains why that matters.

One caution: “beginner” does not mean small or gentle. A Golden Base still grows into a cichlid of 12 inches (30 cm) or more that needs at least 75 gallons as an adult, lives alone, and can be as aggressive as any flowerhorn. Plan the tank before you buy the fish, using our tank setup guide.

Buying and raising a fader

Because you are often buying a fish before its adult color exists, judge what you can see now:

  • Behavior first. An active fish that comes to the glass and eats eagerly is a better bet than a pretty one hiding in the corner.
  • Body and fins. Look for a thick, even body, a straight back, symmetrical fins and clear eyes. None of that changes with the fade.
  • Early gold. Patches of gold or orange already showing through on the belly or face are a good sign of the color to come.
  • Be wary of pushed color. A tiny fish with blazing color may have been fed heavily on color enhancers, and that color can drop off. Our grading guide covers what else to check.

Raising one is standard flowerhorn care: warm, clean water at 80–86°F (27–30°C), big regular water changes, and a quality staple pellet as described in our feeding guide. Color-enhancing foods can brighten red and orange on a faded adult, but they won’t speed up the fade and are no substitute for good water. Questions about Golden Base flowerhorns

Why is my young flowerhorn turning black?

If it is a Golden Base or other fader, going dark is a normal stage before the black fades to gold, yellow or orange. If the fish is also hiding, not eating or clamping its fins, check your water quality, because stress can darken any flowerhorn.

How long does it take a fader flowerhorn to fade?

Usually several months, but keepers report anything from about a month to more than two years. Young fish tend to fade faster, and some pause partway before finishing.

What is the difference between Golden Base and Golden Monkey?

Golden Base is the broad family of gold faders. Golden Monkey originally referred to a specific prized Malaysian line with a chubby face and heavy head pearling, but today the name is often used loosely for any gold flowerhorn.

Is a Golden Base flowerhorn good for beginners?

Yes, it is one of the most common first flowerhorns because it is hardy and affordable. It still needs a large tank of its own and the same care as any adult flowerhorn.

Can I make my flowerhorn fade faster?

Not reliably. Fading is genetic and runs on the fish’s own schedule, so the best you can do is keep the water clean and warm and feed a good staple diet.
